Hague vs. non-Hague countries: apostille or authentication?

Whether you need an apostille or an authentication depends on one thing: whether the country receiving your document is a party to the 1961 Hague Apostille Convention. Here is how to tell, and what each route involves.

The short version

  • Hague country: one certificate, the apostille, from the state office where the document was issued or notarized (or from the U.S. Department of State for federal documents). That is usually the last U.S. step.
  • Non-Hague country: the state office issues an authentication instead. The document then usually needs more steps: certification by the U.S. Department of State, the destination country's embassy or consulate, or both.

What the Convention does

The Apostille Convention replaces the old chain of "legalisation" steps between member countries with a single certificate. An apostille confirms that the signature, seal or stamp on a public document is genuine. It does not confirm that the content is correct.

How to check a country's status

  1. Open the HCCH status table for the Apostille Convention: hcch.net — Apostille Convention status table.
  2. Find the country. A country listed there is a "Contracting Party".
  3. Check the "EIF" (entry into force) date. The HCCH notes that "Contracting Party" includes countries where the Convention has not yet entered into force, so make sure the date has passed.
  4. Look for "A**" in the type column. That marks an accession that another party objected to; the Convention does not apply between those two countries.
  5. Still ask the person or authority receiving the document what they want. They decide what they accept.

The non-Hague route (authentication)

State offices describe the non-Hague route in similar terms. Washington's Secretary of State says documents for countries that are not members "will require additional certification by the U.S. State Department, the country's Embassy, or both" (sos.wa.gov). Maryland's Secretary of State says documents for non-Hague countries are authenticated by the U.S. State Department before going to the country's embassy (sos.maryland.gov). The embassy or consulate of the destination country sets its own rules and fees, so contact it before you start.

Questions and answers

Is China part of the Apostille Convention?

Yes. The HCCH status table shows the Convention in force for China since November 7, 2023.

Is Canada part of the Apostille Convention?

Yes. The HCCH status table shows the Convention in force for Canada since January 11, 2024.

Is the United Arab Emirates part of the Apostille Convention?

The United Arab Emirates was not listed as a Contracting Party on the HCCH status table when we checked on November 13, 2026. Documents for non-member countries usually need an authentication and further certification instead of an apostille.

What happens with a country that is not in the Convention?

State offices issue an authentication instead of an apostille. Washington's Secretary of State, for example, says documents for non-member countries will need additional certification by the U.S. State Department, the country's embassy, or both.

Where do I check a country's status?

The HCCH (Hague Conference on Private International Law) publishes the official status table for the Apostille Convention. When we checked on November 13, 2026, it listed 130 Contracting Parties and was last updated June 30, 2026.
